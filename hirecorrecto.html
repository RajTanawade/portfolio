01 · Overview

Hiring is not one workflow. It’s an ecosystem.

HireCorrecto was not a single-flow product. New hiring workflows, user roles, AI capabilities and technical requirements kept arriving throughout the project.

The design question was never only “what does this screen look like?” It was: does this solution still hold when the next workflow arrives?

03 · Role-Based Experience

7 roles. 1 adaptive product language.

Access is a separate dimension of the product. Different users participate in the same hiring journey with completely different responsibilities.

Instead of creating seven distinct apps, the UI patterns, dashboards, and tables adapt cleanly to each persona’s permission set.

04 · Objectives

Make a complex hiring ecosystem feel like one coherent product.

The platform had to support Super Admin, HR Head, HR Lead, HR, Channel Head, Evaluator and Interviewer — while AI, communication and automation ran across the journey.

Success meant a shared product language: navigation, dashboards, permissions, tables, forms and reporting that could flex by role without becoming seven different products.

06 · Challenges

The product was evolving alongside the business.

New requirements meant new workflows, roles, assessment types, AI capabilities, candidate interactions, dashboards, components — and technical constraints from the libraries already in use.

At the same time, the product needed to compete with established recruitment platforms. Competitive analysis was an ongoing input, not a one-off workshop.

The challenge wasn’t to remove complexity. It was to organize it so people only dealt with what was relevant to their task.

07 · Process & execution

Competitors as a reference point — then a product language of our own.

I used established hiring products to understand navigation, dashboards, candidate management, assessments, interviews, dense data, filtering and configuration. Then I explored multiple layouts against information density, task priority, role context, scalability and whether developers could actually build it on the existing library.

The interesting part: this wasn’t a single benchmarking pass. For almost a year, competitive analysis kept feeding layout decisions as the product expanded.

From UI library to HireCorrecto UI.

I didn’t invent a visual system in isolation. Developers already had libraries. The work was to make stakeholder-facing UI look intentional while staying technically realistic — reuse, adapt, then specify custom variants only when needed, then review in implementation and fold the result back into the DSL.

That closed the gap between what people saw in Figma and what could actually ship.

Scalability is not just reusable components.
