Designing an LMS that helps employees learn, grow and move forward.

01 · Project overview

An internal platform for development, not just course catalogs.

The client wanted an internal Learning Management Platform focused on employee development and career growth. The vision went beyond traditional course management.

I joined as UI/UX Designer, replacing a designer with about six years of experience. The role quickly expanded beyond screens: stakeholder alignment, research, feature definition, design, developer grooming, usability testing, and iteration.

02 · The challenge

Fragmented requirements. Half-cooked features. A product still trying to become an MVP.

When I joined, multiple departments were involved, requirements were hard to align, and several features were only partially defined. The MVP was taking longer than expected. Different features served different personas, with complex role-based access, and the mobile experience needed to become easier for its audience to interpret.

The work was not “design an LMS.” It was: take ownership of ambiguous, competing needs and turn them into experiences the team could actually ship.

05 · Key features

Open a module for the deep-dive. The overview stays short on purpose.

01

Goal setting

Complex hierarchy & role-based UX

The LMS had no goal-setting module. The organization wanted Organization → Department → Team → Individual goals inside the LMS, while hierarchy itself still lived in Workday.

The question

How does my goal contribute to the organization’s goal — without making the interface visually overwhelming?

Users

CEO, department managers, team managers, team leads, senior and junior employees — each with different access.

What I did

Refined stakeholder requirements, researched goal-setting platforms, and designed flows that showed relationship without dumping the full hierarchy on every screen.

The experience let people view organizational and individual goals, understand the mapping, track status, upload evidence of completion, manage approvals by role, and surface completed goals on the employee LMS profile.

Internal validation confirmed that the designed experience met the expected goal-management requirements while keeping a complex multi-level hierarchy understandable for users. There is no quantified usability metric for this module yet.

02

Survey builder

Flexible creation without overwhelm

The LMS had no native survey or feedback system. The aim was a builder that could support LMS, course, pathway and certification feedback — conceptually informed by SurveyMonkey, Google Forms and Microsoft Forms, designed for this ecosystem rather than as a generic form tool.

The tension

Creators needed flexibility and configuration. Respondents needed simplicity and speed. Those are two different products sharing one module.

Users

Creators: admins, L&D, course authors, managers. Respondents: employees and learners.

What I did

Refined requirements, researched comparable platforms, defined flows, designed and prototyped, and iterated with development on feasibility and internal testing.

The module stayed native: creation, question configuration, preview, response collection, and LMS-related feedback use cases — so teams did not have to leave the product to gather input.

The Survey Builder established a native feedback loop within the LMS, enabling feedback around courses, learning pathways, certifications and the overall learning experience. Specific builder mechanics (branching, question types, drag-and-drop) are omitted here unless the shipped UI included them.

03

Reporting analytics

Data visibility + simplicity under a limited DSL

Reporting already existed — but only per tenant. Super Admins had to switch tenant, view a report, switch again, and assemble the organization picture themselves. The Super Admin persona was around 40–50; the dashboard had to stay straightforward, not clever.

Constraints

Large organizational data, and a design system with a limited component set. A new dashboard visual language was not an option.

The principle

Don’t redesign the dashboard when the problem can be solved through better filtering.

What I did

Worked with developers on whether the access model was feasible, then designed around those constraints inside the existing DSL.

Two dropdowns at the top of the existing dashboard: Tenant (All Tenants or a single tenant) and a dependent Subtenant control. Selecting Tenant A exposes A1 / A2 / A3. The same analytics surface then updates to the chosen organizational level.

The solution eliminated the need for Super Admins to switch between tenant accounts to review organizational reporting, bringing consolidated and filtered analytics into a single dashboard. Quantified time or click reductions are not claimed here.

04

Community

Engagement inside a learning product

Community existed so employees would not have to leave the LMS to connect. People used it occasionally, but not often enough — there were too few reasons to return. The answer was not “more social.” It had to become more useful, without turning the LMS into Facebook.

Groups

Created independently around a topic, or linked to a course so learners could ask questions and discuss the same material. Connect with colleagues became learn with colleagues.

Events

Course launches, knowledge-sharing sessions, and author-led conversations — specific learning moments that give people a reason to come back.

Constraints

Implementation friction showed up around group access rights and Zoom for events. Those were real product constraints, not afterthoughts.

The features were developed and tested internally, with positive feedback during testing. Internal testing indicated stronger perceived value and satisfaction with the expanded community experience, particularly by connecting community interactions directly to learning activities. Engagement analytics are a later opportunity, not claimed here.

06 · Process & delivery

Research, then feasibility, then the smallest UI that still solved the problem.

Goal setting and surveys started with competitive and pattern research. Reporting started in grooming: could Super Admins even see across tenants? Community started from an engagement diagnosis, not a feature request list.

Multiple complex features moved from initial concept through design and development within 2–3 sprint cycles. Design and delivery targets were met for the planned market launch.

07 · Learnings

Complexity is a product problem. Screens are how you resolve it.

Joining mid-stream taught me to stabilize requirements before decorating them. Reporting taught me that a small, feasible control can outperform a redesigned dashboard. Community taught me that engagement inside an LMS has to earn its place next to learning, not compete with it.

The through-line: own the ambiguity, stay close to engineering, and ship the version that people can actually use.
